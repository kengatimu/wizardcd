WizardCD — Typography Directions v4

Direction D1 is locked: navy sidebar, crimson accent (now fully visible on dark), Libre Baskerville for headings and page titles. What varies here is the font applied to the content area — table rows, labels, navigation items, supporting text. Ten options, all editorial and established. None of them Inter.

What's being explored: The font applied to table data (app names, statuses, durations), sidebar nav text, and supporting labels. The "Deployment Activity" heading and metric values stay in Libre Baskerville throughout — you already like those.
Direction 01

Lora

— warm editorial serif, 2014

Lora was designed for body text that needs to be read, not scanned. Warm, humanist curves, slight calligraphic influence. "runner-service-ms" in Lora Medium reads like a proper noun in a well-edited document rather than a slug in a config file. This is the closest to Libre Baskerville in character — they work together naturally.

Direction 02

Spectral

— refined digital serif, 2017

Spectral was designed specifically for screen reading — refined, elegant, with an almost Art Deco precision in its thinner weights. At Light or Regular weight it feels like technical documentation from a serious publisher. "eureka-registry-ms" in Spectral reads as a proper noun, not a config slug. This is the most sophisticated and minimal of the serif options.

Direction 03

IBM Plex Serif

— technical publishing serif, 2017

IBM commissioned Plex Serif to pair with their sans-serif in technical documentation. It has a precision and formality that other serifs don't — each letterform is deliberate. In a deployment dashboard it reads as engineering specification rather than editorial copy. "runner-service-ms" in IBM Plex Serif feels like a part number in an IBM manual. Exceptionally readable at small sizes.

Direction 04

Full Baskerville

— complete serif consistency throughout

What if the font you already love for headings was used everywhere — table rows, nav items, all of it? Libre Baskerville was designed by Pablo Impallari for digital use based on the 18th-century Baskerville typeface. When applied to body text it gives the entire UI a unified editorial weight — like reading a well-made annual report where every element is in the same voice.

Direction 05

Vollkorn

— warm oldstyle serif, 2006

Friedrich Althausen's Vollkorn ("wholegrain" in German) is a warm humanist serif designed for body text that needed personality. Where Spectral is refined and Baskerville is authoritative, Vollkorn is warm and organic. The slightly oldstyle numerals and gentle curves make "1m 24s" read like a measurement rather than a timestamp. This direction feels the most human of the serif options.

Direction 06

Bitter

— slab serif, strong and unambiguous

Bitter is a slab serif — heavier, more assertive serifs. Where Spectral whispers authority, Bitter announces it. "runner-service-ms" in Bitter Bold looks like a headline, not an item in a list. This is the choice if you want every app name to feel important. Reuters, Bloomberg Terminal, and Reuters Eikon all use slab serifs for data because they're impossible to misread at a glance. No ambiguity. No squinting.

Direction 07

PT Serif

— formal publishing serif, ParaType 2009

PT Serif was commissioned by the Russian government for official digital publications — that's the provenance. It has a formality and precision that comes from serving as the official typeface for state documents. In a deployment tool this reads as institutional authority. Nothing casual, nothing decorative. App names look like entries in a government register. Completely distinctive from anything in the startup tool space.

Direction 08

Crimson Pro

— book typography, elegant at small sizes

Crimson Pro is book typography — designed for long-form reading in academic and literary publishing. At 12-13px it's exceptionally readable, with delicate contrast between thick and thin strokes. In a deployment table it gives the content a refined, considered quality — like reading footnotes in a scholarly edition. The name fits perfectly: the crimson accent and Crimson Pro body text share the same color vocabulary.

Direction 09

Merriweather

— bold screen serif, heavy authority

Merriweather was designed for maximum readability on screens at any size. Wide letterforms, low-contrast strokes, generous spacing. In a deployment table it makes every row feel substantial and important. App names in Merriweather don't look like config keys — they look like named entities in a contract. This is the boldest and heaviest of the editorial serifs. The New York Times uses Merriweather for body copy. So does The Guardian.

Direction 10

Fraunces

— optical size serif, utterly distinctive

Fraunces is an optical size serif — designed to shift character as it scales, just like traditional metal type. At display sizes the details bloom; at body sizes it settles into dignified readability. The quirky, slightly condensed lowercase letters give it a personality no other font in this list has. "runner-service-ms" in Fraunces looks like the name of a ship, a vessel, something that carries things reliably from one place to another. Which is exactly what it does.
